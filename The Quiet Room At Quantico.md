## Chapter 1: The Speech in the Quiet Room

### What actually happened

On the morning of September 30, 2025, something unprecedented occurred at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Virginia. Roughly 800 of the most senior officers in the United States military — every general, admiral, and senior enlisted leader who could be summoned on short notice — were ordered to assemble. It was the first gathering of its kind in American history: a "key leaders all call" for the entire top command of the armed forces, opened by the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Dan Caine, with no stated agenda beyond an address from the President of the United States and his newly installed Secretary of War, Pete Hegseth.

That morning, before boarding Marine One, President Trump spoke to reporters on the White House lawn. What he said was not private, and it was not subtle: "I'm going to be meeting with generals and with admirals and with leaders. And if I don't like somebody, I'm going to fire them right on the spot."

The officers arrived to find a stage, two podiums, and an atmosphere no one in the room had experienced in decades of service. When the President entered, the room fell silent. Not the silence of respect — the silence of several hundred career officers deciding, in real time, how to comport themselves while their Commander-in-Chief scanned their faces.

Trump noticed. "I never walked into a room so silent before," he told them. "If you want to applaud, you applaud." Then he added the sentence that would be quoted around the world: "If you don't like what I'm saying, you can leave the room. Of course, there goes your rank, there goes your future."

The audience, by every contemporary account — Time, the Associated Press, NPR, PBS — remained silent. Military tradition holds that uniformed personnel do not applaud political speeches, and the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Dan Caine, had reportedly instructed the officers beforehand to maintain the same stoicism they would show at a State of the Union address. But the silence in that room, whatever its cause, was the first fact of the day. The most powerful military leadership corps in the world sat in perfect stillness while the President of the United States told them their careers depended on their enthusiasm.

### What was said

Secretary Hegseth spoke first, for roughly forty minutes, and he came with a list. He announced that the era of the Department of Defense was over — by executive order, signed September 5, 2025, "Restoring the United States Department of War" (a formal statutory rename would require Congress, which has not acted). He announced a new physical-fitness regime: combat troops held to the highest male standard, twice-yearly tests, an end to beard and long-hair waivers. Beyond fitness, he ordered overhauls of the inspector-general, equal-opportunity and equal-advocacy offices — "no more walking on eggshells" — a review of hazing rules, reduced mandatory training, and new social-media policies for the force. He attacked the previous generation of leaders by name — "out with the Chiarellis, the McKenzies and the Milleys" — and declared "no more identity months, DEI offices, dudes in dresses, climate change worship." He told the assembled leaders that if his words made their hearts sink, they should "do the honorable thing and resign."

And then he said the sentence that matters most for everything that follows, the sentence that shows he knew exactly where the legal tripwires were:

"You will win some arguments and you will lose some arguments. But when civilian leaders issue *lawful* orders, we execute. We are professionals in the profession of arms. Our entire constitutional system is predicated upon this understanding."

*Lawful.* One word, carrying the entire weight of the American civil-military compact.

President Trump then spoke for over an hour. His remarks ranged widely — complaints about not receiving the Nobel Peace Prize, stories about battleships, grievances about journalists ("sleazebags") — but threaded through the speech was a consistent and deliberate theme. The transcript, preserved by the American Presidency Project, records these words verbatim:

- "Last month, I signed an Executive Order to provide training for a quick reaction force that can help quell civil disturbances. This is going to be a big thing for the people in this room, because it's the enemy from within, and we have to handle it before it gets out of control."
- "I told Pete, we should use some of these dangerous cities as training grounds for our military. National Guard, but our military. Because we're going into Chicago very soon."
- "We're under invasion from within. No different than a foreign enemy, but more difficult in many ways because they don't wear uniforms. At least when they're wearing a uniform, you can take them out."
- "That's a war too. It's a war from within."

He named cities: Chicago, San Francisco, New York, Los Angeles — "very unsafe places. And we're gonna straighten them out one by one. This is going to be a major part for some of the people in this room."

He referenced the National Guard deployment already underway in Washington, D.C.: "We took out 1,700 career criminals." (This figure was false in important ways — more than 80% of D.C. arrests under the deployment were misdemeanors — but the claim was made to the generals as fact.)

At one point, describing protesters who throw objects at federal vehicles, the President told the room: "You get out of that car, and you can do whatever the hell you want to do."

The speech ended. The officers filed out in silence.

### The reaction

The reaction was immediate and, in the small world of American civil-military relations, volcanic.

Senator Jack Reed of Rhode Island, the ranking Democrat on the Armed Services Committee and a former Army Ranger, called the gathering "an expensive, dangerous dereliction of leadership." Representative Seth Moulton, a Marine veteran, said there was "no 'enemy from within'" and that American cities should never be "training grounds."

Retired Lieutenant General Mark Hertling, who spent decades training the Army, wrote that "trust is gained in drops and lost in buckets." Retired Lieutenant General Russel Honoré, who commanded the Katrina response, criticized the address on ABC the same day. Naureen Shah of the American Civil Liberties Union warned that "military troops must not police us, let alone be used as a tool to suppress the president's critics." And Virginia Burger of the Project on Government Oversight, a federal-accountability nonprofit, noted that the most alarming part was that these words were spoken "to a room full of the people who would most likely be the ones to stand in front of him and tell him no."

Risa Brooks, one of the country's leading scholars of civil-military relations, said the meeting was "part of a larger project aimed at promoting the military leadership's partisan alignment with the administration."

And then, two days later, a retired lieutenant colonel from Ohio named Malcolm Ritchie sat down in front of a camera and recorded his response — the video with which this document opened.

### What Ritchie got right, and what he got wrong

Ritchie was right about the event. The Quantico gathering happened. The audience was largely silent. Trump did say the things about training grounds and the enemy from within. Trump did frame American cities as a battlefield and his domestic opponents as an enemy force. All of that is on the transcript, preserved in the public record.

Ritchie was wrong — significantly wrong — about what Trump said. The President did not say the generals would "wage war against the American people." He did not say troops would be sent "locked and loaded" into neighborhoods to use citizens as "pop-up targets on a live-fire training range." Those phrases are Ritchie's interpretation — vivid, memorable, and false as quotation. They convert a documented statement about "training grounds" for the National Guard into a claim about live-fire military operations against civilians, which nobody in that room was ordered to prepare for and which the transcript does not contain.

His characterization of the president as "cognitively declined" is opinion. His prediction that Trump and Hegseth will end up in a "dark, deep hole" is opinion. His core factual error — calling himself witness to a literal declaration of war on Americans — is the kind of error that lets the other side say: see, it's all hysteria.

But here is the thing the rebuttals miss. You do not need Ritchie's exaggeration to be alarmed by the documented record. The documented record — the actual transcript, the actual executive orders, the actual court rulings, the actual budgets — is sufficient. This document proceeds on the documented record.

And one more fact about Ritchie worth knowing: his own story has a coda that nobody in the viral-video ecosystem mentions. He ran for Congress in Ohio's 6th District as a Democrat in 2026. His platform included "impeach and convict Donald Trump for treason against the Constitution." On May 5, 2026, he finished fifth of six candidates in his own primary, with 9% of the vote. The nomination went to Elizabeth Kirtley, who now faces Republican incumbent Michael Rulli in a district every forecaster rates safe for the GOP. The viral video now circulating under captions calling him a "legendary colonel" — he was a lieutenant colonel — was recorded by a man who was about to lose his own race by twenty-two points. The machinery that amplified him did not mention that either.

## Chapter 2: The Words Before the Words

### The oath everyone swore

To understand why a room full of generals sitting in silence matters, you have to understand what they had all promised.

Every person who puts on the uniform of the United States military swears an oath. It is not the same oath for everyone, and the difference matters.

The enlisted oath, set by federal law at 10 U.S.C. §502, reads: "I will support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic... and that I will obey the orders of the President of the United States and the orders of the officers appointed over me, according to regulations and the Uniform Code of Military Justice."

Read that carefully. The obedience to the President is qualified — "according to regulations and the UCMJ." Lawful orders only.

The officer's oath, set by 5 U.S.C. §3331, contains no mention of the President at all. Officers swear to "support and defend the Constitution" and to "well and faithfully discharge the duties of the office." That's it. No president. No person. The Constitution is the sole object of their allegiance.

This is not an accident. It is the deliberate architecture of the American Republic, designed by people who had watched what happens when armies swear loyalty to men instead of laws.

And woven into military law is the corollary: the duty to *disobey* unlawful orders. Under the Uniform Code of Military Justice, it is a crime to obey an order you know — or should know — is unlawful. Following an unlawful order is not a defense. The standard, refined by courts-martial and military courts over a century and a half, is whether the order is "manifestly unlawful" — whether "a person of ordinary sense and understanding" would know it is illegal. In the words of the 1969 case *United States v. Keenan*, which every military lawyer knows: "The law does not permit a soldier to obey an order that he knows, or should know, is illegal. A soldier is not a robot. He is a reasoning agent."

The Nuremberg tribunals after World War II established the principle for all time: "just following orders" does not excuse atrocity. The Army's own Law of War Manual says it plainly: "An order that violates the law of war is unlawful and must not be obeyed."

So when Ritchie said in his video that his oath was to the Constitution and not to a president — he was right. That is the law. Every officer in the quiet room at Quantico had sworn exactly that oath.

The question this entire document asks is: what happens when the moment arrives to act on it?

### The phrase that took a year to arrive

"Enemy from within" did not originate at Quantico. It has a documented genealogy, and the genealogy matters, because it shows a consistent idea traveling from campaign rhetoric to command rhetoric — the same sentence, delivered to different rooms.

April 27, 2024, in a Time magazine interview, candidate Trump said: "We talk about the enemy from within. I think the enemy from within, in many cases, is much more dangerous for our country than the outside enemies of China, Russia."

May 1, 2024, at a rally in Waukesha, Wisconsin: "I always talk about we have enemies on the outside and we have enemies from within. The enemies from within are more dangerous to our country than China and Russia and others."

October 5, 2024, in Butler, Pennsylvania — the same town where he had survived an assassination attempt months earlier: "We have an enemy from within, which I think is much more dangerous than the outside enemy."

And then, on October 13, 2024 — twenty-three days before the 2024 election — came the formulation that is the direct ancestor of the Quantico speech. In a Fox News interview with Maria Bartiromo, asked about potential Election Day violence, Trump said: "We have some very bad people. We have some sick people, radical left lunatics... and it should be very easily handled by, if necessary, by National Guard or if really necessary, by the military."

"National Guard or if really necessary, by the military." One year later, at Quantico, standing before the military itself: "training grounds for our military. National Guard, but our military." The 2024 promise and the 2025 command are the same sentence. The audience changed. The sentence did not.

Also in October 2024, Trump named individuals as the "enemy from within" — Nancy Pelosi ("she lied; she was supposed to protect the Capitol") and Adam Schiff. Asked by Howard Kurtz whether "enemy from within" was "a pretty ominous phrase," Trump answered: "I think it's accurate."

On October 12, 2024 — one day before the Bartiromo interview — Trump posted a campaign video styled after the film *Full Metal Jacket*, juxtaposing drag performers with military imagery, captioned: "WE WILL NOT HAVE A WOKE MILITARY!" The culture war and the enemy-within frame were fused a full year before Hegseth's directives.

### The first rehearsal

Long before Quantico, there was a dress rehearsal — and it is important because it shows that the institutions this document will track all the way through knew exactly what they were dealing with.

In June 2020, during the protests after George Floyd's murder, President Trump demanded that his defense secretary deploy 10,000 active-duty troops — including the 82nd Airborne — into American streets. He asked, in a meeting recalled by journalists Susan Glasser and Peter Baker, whether the military couldn't simply shoot the protesters: "Can't you just shoot them? Just shoot them in the legs or something?"

Defense Secretary Mark Esper publicly opposed the demand, saying active-duty troops should be used only in "the most urgent and dire of situations." White House Chief of Staff Mark Meadows then called Esper three times to pressure him to recant — an approach one official described as "the Tony Soprano method."

General Mark Milley, then Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, drafted a resignation letter on June 8, 2020. The draft, later obtained and published by The New Yorker, reads in part: "you were doing great and irreparable harm to my country... you are using the military to create fear in the minds of the American people." Milley never sent the letter, on the advice of former Defense Secretary Robert Gates: "Make them fire you. Don't resign. If you go, they all go." Instead, Milley convened the Joint Chiefs, quoted Benjamin Franklin, and — in the reported account — framed any break with the administration as collective: they would "go across the river together," not one by one.

On June 11, 2020, Milley appeared in a video message and apologized for having walked with Trump to the church photo op across Lafayette Square, saying, "I should not have been there."

Four years later, in September 2022, eight former defense secretaries and five former chairmen of the Joint Chiefs — spanning Democratic and Republican administrations going back decades — published an open letter stating the foundational principle in terms that read, in retrospect, like a warning addressed to this exact moment: military officers take an oath to the Constitution "rather than any one individual," and the military has "carefully delimited roles in law enforcement."

### The man at the podium

To understand the Quantico speech, you have to understand Pete Hegseth — and the clearest window into Pete Hegseth is what happened to the lawyers who were supposed to tell him no.

In February 2025, shortly after taking office, Hegseth fired the Judge Advocate General of the Army and the Judge Advocate General of the Air Force. His stated reason, as later reported: they were "roadblocks to orders that are given by a commander in chief." Dozens of retired military lawyers — the Former JAGs Working Group — formed within weeks to document what was happening. The group's founding premise, stated in its first memorandum to Congress, was that the military's legal officer corps was being neutralized precisely because it was the institution whose job was to tell commanders when an order was unlawful.

Hegseth then ordered a "ruthless" review of JAG offices, announced in a March 11, 2026 memorandum — the same memo that floated promoting Judge Advocates General to three-star rank, an incentive lever over the corps. Judge advocates were detailed to serve as immigration prosecutors in civilian federal court — one was held in civil contempt by a Minnesota federal judge after handling 130 cases in a month without ever having practiced in federal court. A March 2026 friend-of-the-court filing — what lawyers call an amicus brief — by former JAGs argued that deploying military lawyers to prosecute civilians in cases with no military nexus violates the Posse Comitatus Act.

The following month, Hegseth issued a memo moving litigation authority from the Judge Advocates General to civilian general counsels — placing military legal advice closer to commanders and further from the uniformed lawyers who administer military justice.

In April 2025, Hegseth also fired Kori Schake from the Defense Policy Board. Schake is one of the country's most respected civil-military scholars — a former National Security Council director, a co-editor with Jim Mattis, the author of the book that this document will quote later as the intellectual counterweight to everything in it. She had served on the board since 2019. Her offense, apparently, was her scholarship.

And at Quantico — one year after the first gathering, on September 30, 2026 — Hegseth returned to address six hundred junior officers and noncommissioned officers in what he called a "State of the Force" address. Trump did not attend. The speech was a victory lap: "Those generals who did not — who clung to the 'Woke Department' or seem to think civilian control of the military is simply a suggestion — well, they no longer work here." He announced a 20% cut in general officer ranks — "the media calls that a purge. I call it accountability" — and the creation of an Office of Religious Affairs, declaring that "the real battle is spiritual" and quoting the Armor of God passage from Ephesians. At one point, reading from his prepared text and carried verbatim in the official transcript as reported by AFP, he described the force he was building: "No fanny — no fatties. No trannies. No beardos. No weirdos. No wimps. No radicals... The ideological clowns are out. The patriotic cowboys are in — with testosterone testing on top. Diversity is not our strength, unity is our strength."

He called media criticism of the military "fundamentally anti-American. It's sick. It's a national betrayal. It's treason, actually" — before conceding, in the same passage, that a free press is important. He announced a new performance-based evaluation for every colonel seeking a general's star: officers "racked and stacked," with the bottom of each cohort cut. And he unveiled six initiatives: an Autonomous Warfare Command, a drone program he called Project Agincourt — "the longbow of this century" — "Fortress America," a competition among states to host "The Next Great American Base" for fifteen to twenty-five thousand troops, an "America's Corps of Cadets" at six universities, and a new "E-6/O-3 rule" elevating the judgment of senior enlisted troops and company-grade officers.

Two weeks after the first Quantico speech, on October 6, 2025, Hegseth issued a memorandum — the "Warrior Ethos Tasking" — ordering every member of the United States military, all 2.1 million of them, to watch a recording of the Quantico address or read its transcript by October 31. ## Chapter 3: The Law of the Oath

### Why "lawful" was the load-bearing word

When Hegseth stood at Quantico and said "when civilian leaders issue *lawful* orders, we execute," he was not being cautious by accident. He was invoking the entire legal architecture that governs the American military — an architecture with deep roots, hard edges, and a body of case law that every officer in that room had studied.

The architecture has several layers, and each one matters to what follows.

**The Posse Comitatus Act** — passed in 1878, in the exhausted aftermath of Reconstruction — makes it a federal crime to use the Army or Air Force (and by regulation, the Navy and Marines) to execute civilian laws, unless the Constitution or an act of Congress expressly authorizes it. The law exists because Americans of that era had direct experience with federal troops acting as a domestic police force — during Reconstruction, federal soldiers enforced voting rights and arrested Klansmen, and when Reconstruction ended, the Southern Democrats who took power wanted those soldiers gone. The law they wrote is the law we still have. Its exceptions are written into other statutes: the Insurrection Act, Title 10 federalization of the National Guard, and a handful of narrow others.

**The Insurrection Act** — dating to 1807, the same year as the statute Jefferson signed against the Burr conspiracy — allows the president to deploy federal troops domestically under specific conditions: an insurrection, domestic violence, unlawful combination, or conspiracy that so hinders the execution of the laws that a class of people is deprived of their constitutional rights, or that impedes the judicial process, or makes it impracticable to enforce federal law by ordinary judicial proceedings. The last time it was invoked was 1992, when President George H.W. Bush sent troops to Los Angeles at the *request* of California's governor, during riots that killed 63 people.

**Title 10, Section 12406** — the modern federalization statute — lets the president call the National Guard into federal service when the United States is invaded, when there is a rebellion or danger of rebellion, or when the president "is unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States."

**The National Guard's dual status.** The Guard is not a simple institution. Normally it belongs to the states — governors command it, and it operates under what is called Title 32 status, in which it is federally funded but state-controlled. The Posse Comitatus Act does not apply to Title 32 Guard in the same way it applies to active-duty forces — a legal feature that matters enormously, as we will see. When the president federalizes the Guard under Title 10, it becomes part of the federal military and the Posse Comitatus Act applies to it fully. And then there is the District of Columbia National Guard, which is different again — its command structure has always been unusual, and that unusualness became a legal battlefield in 2025.

### The doctrine of unlawful orders

The military's internal law is unambiguous about the duty to refuse unlawful orders. The Manual for Courts-Martial presumes orders are lawful — the burden is on the person refusing to show the order was manifestly unlawful. But the presumption has a limit, and the limit is where atrocity begins.

The foundational case is *United States v. Calley*. Lieutenant William Calley ordered his men to murder hundreds of unarmed Vietnamese civilians at My Lai in 1968. At his court-martial, he argued he was following orders. The military court rejected the defense: an order to murder was manifestly unlawful, and Calley had a duty to refuse it. He was convicted.

The 1969 case *United States v. Keenan* added the doctrine's most quoted formulation: "The law does not permit a soldier to obey an order that he knows, or should know, is illegal."

In practice, however, the doctrine has a hard edge in the other direction. Military defense lawyers describe the operational reality as "obey first, question later." Orders are presumed lawful; refusing carries extreme professional risk; and unless an order is "clearly illegal on its face," the advice is to consult counsel first, not refuse. The asymmetry is real: a soldier who refuses a lawful order faces court-martial; a soldier who obeys an unlawful order faces court-martial only if someone with power decides to bring one.

This asymmetry is why the entire apparatus of this story — the firings, the culture-war directives, the mandatory viewing, the QRF — matters. The law gives officers the right to refuse unlawful orders. Everything else in this document describes what happens to the environment in which they would make that decision.

### What the scholars say

The scholarly argument about this moment is not a fringe debate. It is being conducted at the highest level of American academic and policy institutions, and it contains a genuine and important disagreement that this document presents honestly.

**The Schake thesis.** Kori Schake's *The State and the Soldier: A History of Civil-Military Relations in the United States* (2025) — written after Hegseth fired her from the Defense Policy Board — advances an argument that is deliberately uncomfortable for the president's critics. She identifies two stress tests. First: can the president freely dismiss senior officers? Yes — and the military salutes. Second: will officers execute lawful policies they personally oppose? Yes — they deployed over governors' objections, they carried out boat strikes after signing indemnification memos. Her conclusion: "None of this represents a crisis of civilian control. To the contrary, civilian control is working precisely as designed. The crisis is political, and Americans should not expect the military to save them from it."

She identifies a category she calls "lawful but awful" — Insurrection Act threats, Guard federalization over governor objections — and argues the military "largely will, and should, carry these out." Her warning is to the people asking the military to be the solution: "Not only can our military not save American democracy, it can't even save itself from democracy."

Her most pointed criticism is aimed at both sides: when Senator Mark Kelly and other lawmakers made a video telling troops they could refuse unlawful orders, Schake argued, they made law-compliance itself "seem like a political act." Her endgame warning: each politicization absorbed in silence makes the next easier — producing not an insubordinate military, but "an increasingly partisan military" recruited and shaped under conditions the law cannot catch.

**The countercurrent.** Lindsay Cohn of the Naval War College argues the opposite risk: purges of senior officers *reduce* the probability of a coup but *increase* the probability of compliance with bad orders. Heidi Urben, in the journal *Dædalus* (Fall 2025), writes that politicization "occurs within the larger context of democratic backsliding," and that "the military is the state's legitimate instrument of violence. When this instrument becomes politicized or is perceived to be politicized, it undermines the very foundation of democratic governance." Rosa Brooks, in the same issue, argues the civil-military-relations paradigm itself is insufficient for the current threat.

**The generals' letters.** On January 3, 2021 — after the election, before the inauguration — all ten living former defense secretaries published an open letter in the Washington Post. It was born, by report, from a conversation between Dick Cheney and former undersecretary Eric Edelman. It stated: "Efforts to involve the U.S. armed forces in resolving election disputes would take us into dangerous, unlawful and unconstitutional territory," and warned that "civilian and military officials who direct or carry out such measures would be accountable, including potentially facing criminal penalties."

That letter was the first. It was followed by the 89-defense-officials letter of June 2020, the ten-retired-officials letter backing Harris in September 2024, the former service secretaries' letter of February 2025 protesting the firings of General Brown and the service chiefs, the amicus briefs from retired officers in the Los Angeles case (September 2025, eleven signers), the D.C. case (December 2025, thirty-plus signers), the Kelly case (April 2026, seventy-two signers), and — on October 6, 2026, one month before this writing — a video by seven retired senior leaders (Hayden, McChrystal, Lute, Hertling, Clapper, Quagliotti, Bolden) urging election protection in nonpartisan terms.

The same institution. Five years. An escalating sentence, every time the line moved. In November 2025, a coalition of veterans organizations — under an ACLU umbrella — demanded that Congress pull the troops from American cities and restrict future deployments, exercising the power of the purse.

### General Caine

The man who now holds the office Milley held — Chairman of the Joint Chiefs — is General Dan Caine, and his file matters.

Caine is an Air Force pilot with 2,800 flight hours, one of the four fighter pilots who flew the D.C. air-defense sortie on September 11, 2001 — he reportedly told his wingman, "Don't shoot anybody. I'll make the decision." He served at JSOC — the Joint Special Operations Command, the military's most secretive operational headquarters — commanded air operations in Iraq and Syria, spent three years at the CIA as associate director for military affairs, and retired in 2024.

Trump's account of meeting him is famous in MAGA circles: he says Caine told him, "Sir, I'll kill for you, sir," and that Caine wore a MAGA hat. Caine has denied both — "I think the president was actually talking about somebody else." Trump's story, as told at CPAC — the Conservative Political Action Conference, the movement's flagship gathering — appears nowhere in the official record.

Caine's nomination was unprecedented: the first Chairman never to have held four-star rank before, the first nominated from retirement, the first from the reserve component, the first Air National Guardsman — requiring a legal waiver of statutory requirements. He was confirmed 60–25, with every Democratic vote against him.

## Chapter 4: The Deployments

### Los Angeles: June 2025

The first deployment of the era came fast. On June 7, 2025 — as protests against raids by Immigration and Customs Enforcement — ICE, the federal deportation agency — in Los Angeles escalated — President Trump federalized 2,000 California National Guard troops over the explicit objection of Governor Gavin Newsom, the first time since 1965 that a president activated a state's Guard without the governor's consent. Seven hundred Marines followed on June 9. The administration cited Title 10, Section 12406: an alleged inability of civilian law enforcement to execute the laws.

The courts responded. Judge Charles Breyer of the Northern District of California ruled on September 2, 2025, that the deployment violated the Posse Comitatus Act. His opinion is worth quoting, because it is one of the most important judicial statements of this era: the administration's apparent aim, he wrote, was "creating a national police force with the President as its chief." The premise was false: "There was no rebellion, nor was civilian law enforcement unable to respond." And the administration had "knowingly contradicted their own training materials, which listed twelve functions that the Posse Comitatus Act bars."

The Ninth Circuit — the federal appeals court covering the western states — held that the statute's "unable" requirement means substantially impaired — not literally incapable — and allowed the deployment to continue in part. But it also established something crucial: the president's emergency declarations are reviewable by courts. The administration had claimed they were not. The court said they were. The administration lost that point, permanently.

Retired defense officials and former service secretaries filed amicus briefs for California. Internal military memos, later documented, assessed the deployment as posing "extremely high" risk to civilians, troops, and the military's reputation, with "far-reaching social, political and operational" impacts. A hundred and five Guard members sought behavioral-health counseling. At least one company commander and one battalion commander who objected were reassigned. Troops called it a "fake mission."

Judge Breyer ultimately ordered the California Guard returned to Governor Newsom. The troops were off the streets by December 31, 2025. The total cost, according to the Congressional Budget Office: $193 million.

### Washington, D.C.: August 2025 and the city that never asked

On August 11, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order 14333, invoking for the first time ever a provision of the Home Rule Act that allows the president to assume control of the D.C. Metropolitan Police Department in an emergency. Eight hundred D.C. National Guard troops were activated; the force grew to approximately 2,600, drawn from as many as eleven states. The troops were deputized by the U.S. Marshals Service — an unusual arrangement that gave them authority to detain — and were issued sidearms, also unusual for domestic deployment.

The deployment was framed as a crime emergency. (The federal takeover of the Metropolitan Police Department itself expired on September 10, 2025; the Guard deployment continued separately, on its own legal track.) The president claimed to have "taken out 1,700 career criminals." The data showed something different: more than 80% of arrests were misdemeanors, and multiple independent analyses found no measurable effect on violent crime — the declines that occurred tracked opportunistic crime like petty theft, and the city's crime had already been falling. The public's verdict was lopsided: a Washington Post/Schar School poll in August 2025 found 80% of D.C. residents opposed the federalization, and a national Reuters/Ipsos poll the same month found 46% opposed to the Guard deployments against 38% in support — 80% of Democrats opposed, 12% of Republicans.

On November 26, 2025 — the day before Thanksgiving — a gunman ambushed two West Virginia National Guard members three blocks from the White House. Specialist Sarah Beckstrom, twenty years old, died the next day; she was buried on December 9 at the national cemetery in Grafton, West Virginia. Staff Sergeant Andrew Wolfe was critically wounded — he underwent skull reconstruction in March 2026 and is recovering. The gunman, Rahmanullah Lakanwal, was a twenty-nine-year-old Afghan national who had served with a CIA-backed paramilitary unit during the war, entered the U.S. through Operation Allies Welcome — the Biden-era resettlement program for Afghan wartime allies — in 2021, and had been granted asylum in April 2025 — during the Trump administration. Investigators never established a motive. His own defense attorneys noted that "the government has proffered no motive evidence" and that "all the known evidence of Mr. Lakanwal's allegiances show his solidarity with U.S. military personnel."

The administration's response fused the deployment politics with the immigration politics: the president ordered five hundred additional Guard troops to D.C., demanded reexamination of all Biden-era Afghan arrivals, paused all Afghan immigration processing, called for a "permanent pause" on migration from "third world countries," and extended the deployment through February 2026, then through 2026, then — in July 2026 — through January 20, 2029, "or until terminated by the President."

The District of Columbia sued. On November 20, 2025, Judge Jia Cobb of the D.C. federal district court ruled that the deployment "likely violated" the D.C. Code, which permits Guard deployment in the city only at the request of the D.C. government — a request that was never made. Her opinion contains the cleanest judicial description of the administration's legal method: the government's reading of the statute "would obviate the need to satisfy the predicates found in Title 10 and the Insurrection Act and provide an escape hatch from the PCA, allocating power to the President without any corresponding checks."

The D.C. Circuit — the federal appeals court seated in the capital — stayed her ruling on December 17, 2025 — but on standing, not merits. Judge Patricia Millett authored the stay; Judges Neomi Rao and Gregory Katsas concurred, questioning whether D.C., as a federal district created by Congress, could sue at all. The merits of Judge Cobb's ruling — that the deployment violates the law — have never been reversed. They simply haven't been reached.

As this is written, the case is fully briefed and **oral argument is calendared for tomorrow, Wednesday, October 14, 2026** — twenty days before the election, in a legal cluster that also includes a federal court hearing the same day on whether a records agency feigned compliance with a public-records injunction. The troops are still there. The Pentagon's own projection for the mission's cost is $1.429 billion over fiscal years 2027–29. The Senate Homeland Security Committee's Democratic staff calculated $602 million annualized — more than the D.C. police department's entire budget. Taxpayers for Common Sense calculated the per-soldier cost at $647 per day; the group's running tally passed $473 million by mid-November 2025.

The city's own records, obtained through public-records requests, document an effect no one anticipated when the troops arrived: in the deployment's first two weeks, commuting fell 11.5% and non-resident visits fell 22%. The economic life of the capital receded from its own military presence. And by April 2026, an Associated Press dispatch described troops among the cherry blossoms collecting trash and removing graffiti — "barely mentioned in city council meetings or by candidates." The deployment had become scenery.

On July 4, 2026 — the nation's 250th birthday, the largest Salute to America ever staged, 850,000 fireworks shells — the interim commander of the D.C. Guard told reporters his troops would "continue the roles they have served the last 10 months." By then the force had grown to roughly 5,150 troops — the Marshals Service's requested reinforcement having been approved for the anniversary — at a cost the Senate Homeland Security Committee's minority staff put at $332 million for the first seven months, a $602 million annualized run rate.

The attorney general litigating the case, Brian Schwalb, won renomination in June 2026 with more than ninety percent of the vote; his campaign platform pledges to "stop the illegal National Guard deployment." He faces Republican Manuel Rivera on November 3. The lawyer carrying the era's defining case is also on the ballot.

 The largest military celebration in American history was also, quietly, the normalization of the largest domestic military deployment in modern American history.

### Portland: the judge who said no permanently

On September 27, 2025, President Trump ordered 200 Oregon National Guard troops to Portland — after a Fox News segment aired archival 2020 footage. The troops staged outside the city and never deployed. But the legal fight mattered.

On November 7, 2025, Judge Karin Immergut — a Trump appointee — permanently barred the deployment. Her opinion reached back to the founding: "this country has a longstanding and foundational tradition of resistance to government overreach, especially in the form of military intrusion into civil affairs... a nation of Constitutional law, not martial law." The protests, she found, were "predominately peaceful" and had "quickly abated." The deployment "did not have a lawful basis."

The troops were withdrawn by January 2026. The cost for a deployment that never entered the city it was ordered to secure: $21 million.

### Chicago and Illinois: the Supreme Court weighs in

The Illinois deployment produced the era's defining legal confrontation. The run-up to Chicago had its own documented record. Operation Midway Blitz — the ICE surge that produced more than three thousand arrests — began on September 8, 2025. On September 6, an official account posted an AI-generated riff on Apocalypse Now: "I love the smell of deportations in the morning... Chicago is about to find out why it's called the Department of WAR." Governor J.B. Pritzker responded to the federalization threats directly: "The President of the United States is threatening to go to war with an American city. This is not a joke. This is not normal." On October 4–5, 2025, the president federalized 300 Illinois Guard troops and 200 Texas Guard troops over Pritzker's objection, claiming a "rebellion or danger of rebellion" at the Broadview, Illinois facility, where protests had been met with tear gas and where a federal judge later restricted the use of riot-control weapons.

Judge April Perry of the Northern District of Illinois issued a temporary restraining order on October 9, 2025: "no credible evidence that there is a danger of rebellion in the state of Illinois." The federalized troops spent their entire deployment at Marseilles, Illinois — 81 miles from Chicago — and at Joliet, 53 miles away. They never entered the city.

The Seventh Circuit, the federal appeals court for the Midwest, upheld the block: "political opposition is not rebellion."

On December 23, 2025, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3. The unsigned opinion — issued "for the court as a whole," what lawyers call a per curiam — was narrow and devastating for the administration: "At this preliminary stage, the Government has failed to identify a source of authority that would allow the military to execute the laws in Illinois." Justice Kavanaugh concurred on narrower grounds. Justices Alito and Thomas dissented in a sixteen-page opinion; Justice Gorsuch dissented separately.

The majority's reasoning built a wall for the future. The statute's phrase "regular forces," they held, means the actual U.S. military — and because the Posse Comitatus Act bars using the regular military for domestic law enforcement absent an exception, the president would need something like the Insurrection Act to deploy in Illinois, and would need to show he is "unable" to enforce the laws with civilian forces. He had shown neither.

Justice Gorsuch's dissent planted the seed of the next fight: "What, if any, inherent Article II power does the President have to deploy the Guard to protect federal personnel or property... and if, as all parties seem to assume, today's Guard is the successor to the militia of the founding era, how far can this inherent Presidential authority extend before it intrudes on Congress's powers?"

Justice Kavanaugh added a footnote that scholars immediately recognized as an invitation: the ruling "could cause the President to use the U.S. military more than the National Guard." Professor Paul Gowder of Northwestern called it "an invitation for Trump to go straight to the Insurrection Act."

On December 31, 2025, the president withdrew from Chicago, Los Angeles, and Portland simultaneously. His accompanying message read: "We will come back, perhaps in a much different and stronger form."

On April 20, 2026, Judge Perry dismissed the Illinois case with prejudice as moot, relying on a January 2026 Department of Defense memorandum directing demobilization. Her dismissal contains the line that may outlast everything else in the opinion: "The court can no longer provide ongoing protection against hypothetical unlawful acts committed by the federal government."

### Minnesota: the surge and the shootings

The deployment era's most violent chapter began on November 29, 2025, when ICE launched Operation Metro Surge in Minneapolis — a force of 2,000 officers that grew to roughly 3,000, five times the size of the entire Minneapolis Police Department.

On January 7, 2026, an ICE agent shot and killed Renee Good, a thirty-seven-year-old mother of three, in her car. DHS initially called her a "domestic terrorist"; video contradicted the account. On January 13, a second non-fatal shooting.

On January 15, 2026, the president posted: "If the corrupt politicians of Minnesota don't obey the law and stop the professional agitators and insurrectionists from attacking the Patriots of I.C.E.... I will institute the INSURRECTION ACT."

The next day, he "backed off a bit," as the Associated Press reported.

But on January 18, 2026, the Department of Defense ordered approximately 1,500 active-duty soldiers — two battalions of the 11th Airborne Division, based in Alaska — to prepare for possible deployment to Minneapolis. The order was issued over a holiday weekend. The troops were recalled to base as a "quick reaction force." No mission was ever outlined. A second standby order followed on January 20 for a military-police brigade at Fort Bragg.

This was the closest the era came to crossing its own red line: active-duty regulars, prepared for deployment against American civilians, on the president's Insurrection Act threat. It was also, as far as the documented record shows, the first preparation of active-duty ground forces for domestic deployment since 1992.

On January 24, 2026, it ended in blood. Alex Pretti, a thirty-seven-year-old intensive-care nurse at a Veterans Affairs hospital, was shot and killed by agents of Customs and Border Protection, CBP, during a protest. Bystander video — verified by Reuters, the BBC, the Wall Street Journal, and the Associated Press — shows an agent removing Pretti's licensed handgun from its holster approximately one second before the shots. At least ten shots were fired in five seconds, some as Pretti lay motionless on the ground. The president initially called him "an agitator and, perhaps, insurrectionist." Gun-rights groups — including the Minnesota Gun Owners Caucus — pushed back. On January 30, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche announced a federal civil-rights investigation, with the FBI leading.

On January 30, 2026, a national general strike was called.

The 1,500 active-duty troops stood down over the weekend of January 31–February 1, 2026. The New York Times broke the stand-down. Kristi Noem, by then under fire, announced that ICE officers in Minneapolis would begin wearing body cameras. By February 18, 2026, the 11th Airborne was back in Alaska, executing arctic airborne training at Fairbanks — restored to its Pacific deterrence mission within a month of being recalled as a domestic quick reaction force. Their commander, Brigadier General John Cogbill, declined an invitation from the Alaska state legislature to discuss the stand-down episode; a Republican state representative wrote to urge him not to appear.

The Minnesota litigation produced the era's most significant judicial findings — the 96 violated court orders, the due-process findings, the First Amendment injunction — which are detailed in Chapter 11. As of this writing, the Pretti investigation continues. No charges have been filed. Governor Tim Walz, Attorney General Keith Ellison, and Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey all responded to the Insurrection Act threat with explicit legal warnings; Ellison stated he would sue.

### Memphis: the state-law track

The Memphis deployment ran on a different legal track — state authorization rather than federal — and it produced the era's first civilian killing by National Guard gunfire.

The Memphis Safe Task Force was announced on September 12, 2025, with a presidential memorandum following. Governor Bill Lee, a Republican, was cooperative: approximately 700 Tennessee Guard troops patrolled the city from October 2025 in "military police" vests, carrying holstered weapons but without arrest power. Stephen Miller told officers they were "unleashed." The city government and the Shelby County Board opposed the deployment; the board deadlocked on a resolution, refusing either to support or condemn it.

On July 5, 2026, at approximately 4 a.m. in downtown Memphis, police responded to a shots-fired call and began a foot pursuit of Tyrin Johnson, a twenty-year-old Tennessee State student. Two National Guard members joined the pursuit. Both fired. Johnson was killed.

The official account: Johnson "turned toward NG members with his weapon." The family, briefed by the Tennessee Bureau of Investigation, was told he was shot twice in the chest. The Shelby County Medical Examiner ruled the death a homicide — the bullet entered his back and pierced his lung. A community organizer who said she had seen video described Johnson running from an unmarked car, hearing shots, and Johnson falling: "You don't see Tyrin turn around. You don't see anything in his hands."

One infrastructure fact reframes every competing account: the Tennessee National Guard in Memphis did not own or wear body cameras.

The family demanded the release of the SkyCop camera footage. The Tennessee Bureau of Investigation continues to investigate. No charges have been filed. The NAACP sent a letter to the Justice Department requesting a federal investigation. The Memphis deployment's defenders cited a 44% drop in murders; the city's own data showed crime had been declining before the troops arrived.

By mid-2026, four people had been killed by federal authorities under the Memphis task force; a fifth died in the Johnson shooting. Judge Patricia Head Moskal of the Tennessee chancery court ruled in November and December 2025 that the state-law predicates for the deployment did not exist — there was no written request from the Trump administration and no written order from Governor Lee, only press releases; "neither 'grave emergency' nor 'disaster' can fairly be construed to include 'ongoing criminal activity.'" The Tennessee Court of Appeals reversed on standing in April 2026. The Guard remains.

### New Orleans: the consent model

One deployment ran differently. Louisiana's Republican governor, Jeff Landry, requested Guard assistance in New Orleans in September 2025; 350 troops deployed in December 2025. The New Year's Eve patrol covered the French Quarter only, with no immigration enforcement function. The administration cites New Orleans as the template. The courts have never had occasion to review it, because no one objected. This is the consent model — the one the administration's own lawyers have cited since the Supreme Court's Illinois ruling narrowed the federalization path.

### The pattern

Across every deployment, the same sequence repeats: a presidential claim of emergency; a governor's objection where the governor is a Democrat; a court finding the factual predicate absent; a legal-status change that keeps the troops in place or brings them back; and a normalization that makes the next deployment easier to order. The status changes are documented and specific: Title 32 instead of Title 10 in D.C.; the Home Rule Act's emergency provision instead of Section 12406; state consent instead of federalization in Memphis and New Orleans; the Immigration and Nationality Act — the ordinary immigration law — instead of the Alien Enemies Act for removals. Each change is lawful on its face. Each one answers a court ruling without obeying it.

## Chapter 5: The Execution

### How a military execution became a spectacle

On December 3, 2026 — fifty-two days from this writing, and thirty days after the midterm election — the United States Army intends to execute Nidal Hasan by firing squad at Fort Hood, Texas, at 1 p.m. Central Time. The date, hour, and location were chosen to mirror the November 5, 2009 attack, in which then-Major Hasan killed thirteen people and wounded dozens more at the same installation. Hasan was convicted by a military jury in August 2013 and sentenced to death. In March 2025, the Supreme Court denied his final direct appeal. The Army's execution planning — reported under the codename Operation Resolute Justice — began months before presidential approval.

Under military law, a president must personally approve a military execution. On October 2, 2026, President Trump approved Hasan's sentence. On October 6, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle signed the execution order: firing squad, Fort Hood, December 3, 1 p.m. — the same weekday, hour, and location as the attack.

This would be the first U.S. military execution since 1961. It would be the first military use of the firing squad since Private Eddie Slovik was executed in January 1945 — the only American soldier shot for desertion in World War II, a story so shameful that the Army kept it secret for a decade. And it would be the first public execution in the United States since Rainey Bethea was hanged before a crowd of twenty thousand in Owensboro, Kentucky, in 1936.

Because the Pentagon confirmed, through the Associated Press, that the execution will be **livestreamed**.

On October 8, 2026, a Pentagon official told reporters: "The Secretary is correct. It will be livestreamed. Details will follow."

The announcement was made through the media ecosystem of Jack Posobiec's "Human Events Daily" — the same day Hegseth was aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln in San Diego. His words: "No brainer. An army major betrays his country, through radical Islamism. Kills a bunch of Americans at Fort Hood, of course we're going to do it at Fort Hood... Don't care. We'll see him on December 3."

### The legal machinery, and the gap in it

Hasan's attorneys — led by John Galligan, a retired Army colonel and former military judge — were notified late at night, without counsel present. Galligan said a habeas corpus petition — the ancient legal mechanism by which a prisoner can demand that a court justify his detention — is "a distinct possibility." Eugene Fidell of Yale, the country's leading military-law scholar, noted that habeas could delay the execution for years — but also noted that Hasan might decline to seek it, if martyrdom is his preference. The precedent is Ronald Gray, whose 2008 military execution was stayed and has been in legal limbo ever since.

Here is the anomaly that may matter most. According to ABC News' internal documents, the Army spent months preparing to execute Hasan by lethal injection. The firing-squad order forced the abandonment of that plan. And the approval came days after what happened in Tennessee — which must be told first.

### Christa Pike, the execution that failed

On September 30, 2026, the state of Tennessee attempted to execute Christa Pike, who would have been the first woman executed in the state in more than two hundred years. The execution failed. Two full doses of pentobarbital were administered. At least seven needle insertion attempts, one needle bending. A witness described unconsciousness accompanied by choking sounds that "went on for so long." The execution team passed through six locked doors before reaching a telephone. The Tennessee Supreme Court's number went to voicemail. The Supreme Court lifted the stay at 7 p.m.; Justice Sotomayor dissented in a three-page opinion, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, writing that there was "no reason to short-circuit ordinary appellate review."

Christa Pike survived. She is the only person in American capital-punishment history known to have survived a complete execution attempt. Governor Bill Lee halted all remaining 2026 Tennessee executions, called the episode "deeply disturbing," accepted the resignation of the Tennessee Department of Correction chief, and appointed an independent review.

The review revealed another fact: the same doctor was present at both Tennessee botches — Mark Walton Fowler, who had also attended the failed Carruthers execution in May. No protocol was changed between them.

On October 7, a judge issued an evidence-preservation order. On October 10, Pike was discharged back to prison.

Two days after the Pike attempt, Trump approved Hasan's execution. Four days after that, the Army switched to the firing squad — a method for which, as ABC's documents showed, the Army had no prepared protocol. The two execution threads are one decision tree: a botched injection, a hasty method change, and an execution date set for the earliest moment regulations permit, at a venue chosen for its symbolism, announced through friendly media, from the deck of an aircraft carrier, to be carried live.

### The reactions — and the contradiction inside the administration

Senator Mark Kelly, a retired Navy captain and astronaut, said: "There is no doubt that Nidal Hasan is a murderer. He should face justice for that. But a public execution? Here in the United States? It's the kind of thing Iran and the Taliban do." Kelly also called for Hegseth's removal.

Representative Austin Scott, a Republican: live-streaming "is what jihadists do. America is better than that."

Senator John Curtis, a Republican: "a civilization erodes when it cultivates an appetite for executions as a form of entertainment."

Joe Kent — the former National Counterterrorism Center chief who had resigned in March 2026 over the Iran war — called it "a stain on our morality."

Geoffrey Corn, a former JAG and professor at Texas Tech: "turning the execution... into some type of public spectacle echoes of the past we seem to have put behind us."

The United Nations human rights office said a livestreamed execution would be "tantamount to a public execution... would breach the absolute prohibition of torture." Pope Leo XIV, on the World Day Against the Death Penalty, called the death penalty "inadmissible." The U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops called it "barbaric."

And then, the administration contradicted itself. Vice President JD Vance, asked about the livestream on October 10, said: "I don't know that that's actually going to happen." The president, asked by ABC the same day, said "they're making a determination about that." The livestream confirmed by the Pentagon on October 8 was being walked back on October 10 — the first public split inside the administration on a military-justice question.

The regulatory gap is documented. Army Regulation 190-55 — the governing regulation — contains no procedure for livestreaming. The Army must either amend its regulations or seek an exemption. As of this writing, no habeas petition has been filed. The Army has not announced a livestream protocol.

The man who signed the execution order is the Acting Secretary of the Army, Adam Telle — a non-veteran policy specialist from Alabama who spent a decade as Senator Thad Cochran's top national-security aide, then ran the Senate Appropriations Homeland Security Subcommittee's staff — the committee that wrote the DHS money — then served as Assistant Secretary of the Army for Civil Works, where Senator Sheldon Whitehouse accused him of "the worst politicization of the Corps of Engineers in modern times." He became Acting Secretary in September 2026, bypassing the undersecretary, after Dan Driscoll's resignation. One person has now touched all three threads: the money that built the detention system, the Corps infrastructure decisions, and the most controversial military-justice order in sixty-five years.

## Chapter 6: The Machinery of the "Enemy"

### The Antifa designation — built on nothing, pointed at everything

On September 17, 2025 — one week after Charlie Kirk's assassination — President Trump signed an executive order: "Designating Antifa as a Domestic Terrorist Organization." The order calls Antifa "a militarist, anarchist enterprise that explicitly calls for the overthrow of the United States Government."

There is a problem with this, and it is not political. It is legal. There is no statutory authority to designate a domestic organization as a terrorist organization. The Brennan Center's analysis called the order "ungrounded in fact and law... the purported designation has no legal effect." FBI Director Christopher Wray had said in 2020 that antifa is an ideology, not an organization. The Congressional Research Service agreed. Eight days after the order, on September 25, 2025, the White House issued National Security Presidential Memorandum-7, titled "Countering Domestic Terrorism and Organized Political Violence." ### The Former JAGs Working Group

The most detailed legal analysis of where this machinery points came from an organization that did not exist eighteen months ago.

The Former JAGs Working Group — several dozen retired military lawyers from all services, organized under retired Major General Steven Lepper and formed in February 2025 after the TJAG firings, hosted by the nonpartisan Protect Democracy — has produced seven formal memoranda to Congress, essays, and statements. Their sixth memorandum, dated April 21, 2026, is titled "The Insurrection Act and Military Personnel at Polling Places," and it is the single most important legal document in this file.

Its thesis: "The President is building a case to deploy troops, ICE, or some other armed federal paramilitary force to influence the elections in November."

The memo lays out the connect-the-dots chain with citations. First, the Antifa designation creates the justification. Second, a 2001 Office of Legal Counsel opinion held that military action against domestic terrorism counts as "protecting the nation's security, rather than executing the laws" — and therefore does not violate the Posse Comitatus Act. Third, if Antifa can be designated an "armed enemy," the prohibition on military at polling places — 18 U.S.C. §592, which makes it a crime to station armed federal forces at voting locations "unless such force be necessary to repel armed enemies of the United States" — could be circumvented. Fourth, the administration's election-related executive orders create the predicate: the March 25, 2025 order requiring proof of citizenship for voting and conditioning federal funds on cooperation; the March 31, 2026 order restructuring mail-in voting via USPS and creating a national eligible-voter list. States that ignore these orders could be characterized as "refusing" to enforce federal law and "depriving" voters of rights — the exact language of 10 U.S.C. §253, the Insurrection Act provision for states that "fail to protect" their people.

The memo quotes past-is-prologue precedents: the National Defense Area executive order along the border, declared under "mass migration national emergency" authority, which created Posse Comitatus-free zones; the fentanyl-as-WMD designation, which opened domestic military-use authorities.

## Chapter 7: The Force That Was Built

### The 23,000

On August 25, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14339, titled "Additional Measures to Address the Crime Emergency in the District of Columbia." Section 4(b) of that order directed the Secretary of Defense to establish a "standing National Guard quick reaction force" available "for rapid nationwide deployment."

On September 24, 2025, Hegseth signed the implementing memorandum: "National Guard Domestic Civil Disturbance and Law Enforcement Training and Domestic Response Force."

On October 8, 2025, Major General Ronald Burkett, the National Guard Bureau's director of operations, issued the implementation guidance. It leaked to The Guardian. The numbers: approximately 23,500 troops — five hundred per state, with smaller contingents for Delaware, Alaska, and Guam. Each state would receive "one hundred sets of crowd control equipment" — tasers, pepper spray, batons, body shields. Pentagon trainers would run courses in October, November, and December. Each state would report monthly readiness through the Defense Readiness Reporting System. The deadline: operational no later than January 1, 2026.

A mobilized guardsman told Task & Purpose that the new gear and training were "taking it up a level... what you would need for hasty checkpoints and for detainee operations... much more extensive than what we're generally doing when we're training to assist civilian authorities."

On October 31, 2025, the signed National Guard Bureau implementation memorandum went into the official record. As of this writing, no award has been publicly identified. When the executive order surfaced, legal experts reached for superlatives: Mark Nevitt, an Emory professor and former Navy JAG, said he saw "nothing that will legally prohibit what the president is seeking" — the Title 32 hybrid being a Posse Comitatus "loophole" — and the Brennan Center's Joseph Nunn warned that the order "makes it easy to deploy military personnel anywhere, at any time, for any purpose."

## Chapter 8: The Argument

### Schake's warning, taken seriously

It would be easy — and wrong — to write this document as a simple story of a president breaking the law and a military refusing. The record is more complicated, and the complication matters.

Kori Schake's book, and the broader scholarly consensus she represents, makes an argument that every reader of this document should sit with: civilian control of the military is working. The president fired generals, and the military saluted. The president ordered deployments over governors' objections, and the troops went. The president ordered boat strikes, and officers signed indemnification memos and carried them out. None of that is a broken system. That is the system.

Her conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable: "The military cannot save us from the political leaders Americans elect. And we should not want them to."

If that is right — and the evidence in this document largely supports it — then the responsibility lies where the Constitution put it: with Congress, with the courts, with the states, and with the voters. The military's job is to refuse unlawful orders and obey lawful ones, even awful ones. The rest of us cannot outsource the defense of the Republic to people we have trained to follow.

And yet. The same record shows what happens to the environment in which those lawful-or-unlawful judgments get made. The JAGs who would advise soldiers are fired, detailed away, or moved under civilian control. The promotion boards are reshaped. The mandatory-viewing order makes a political speech compulsory. The fitness standards are enforced selectively — seven Texas troops pulled for their weight, while the generals who authorized the deployment that put them there kept their ranks. The commander who declines to discuss a domestic standby order with his own state legislature receives a supportive letter from a state representative telling him not to appear.

The question is not whether the military will save the Republic. The question is whether the Republic is making it impossible for the military's own internal safeguards — the oath, the JAGs, the presumption-of-lawfulness doctrine, the manifestly-unlawful standard — to function as designed. And on that question, the evidence of this document is less comforting.

### The history that argues back

There is one more set of facts that belongs in this argument, because it is the history the president's defenders cite and the history his critics misremember.

When federal troops have entered American cities before, the circumstances were these: In 1957, a reluctant President Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne to Little Rock — not to enforce his own policy, but to enforce a federal court order against a governor who was using the Arkansas National Guard to block nine Black children from a school. Eisenhower told the nation explicitly: "Federal troops are not being used to relieve local and state authorities of their primary duty to preserve the peace and order of the community." In 1962, President Kennedy federalized the Mississippi Guard to enforce a court order admitting James Meredith to Ole Miss. In 1965, President Johnson federalized the Alabama Guard to protect the Selma-to-Montgomery marchers after Governor Wallace refused. In 1967, President Johnson sent troops to Detroit at the governor's request. In 1992, President Bush sent troops to Los Angeles after Governor Wilson requested them, during riots that killed sixty-three people, with the mayor of Los Angeles asking for the regular Army by name.

In every case, one of two things was true: the governor requested the troops, or the troops were enforcing court orders against state officials who were defying the Constitution by obstructing rights.

The 2025–26 record matches neither pattern. The governors objected. The courts found the predicates absent — "no rebellion," "predominately peaceful," "no credible evidence." The deployments proceeded on legal theories that five separate courts found wanting. And the mission was not the enforcement of anyone's constitutional rights. It was immigration enforcement and crime politics — carried out, in city after city, by soldiers trained for war.

### What a colonel got right

Return, in closing this section, to the man from Dover, Ohio.

Malcolm Ritchie embellished. He took documented words and inflated them into declarations of war. He was wrong to do it, and the inflation damaged the true case. But embedded in his exaggeration was a fact that no fact-check has touched: he understood the oath correctly. He said, on camera, that his oath was to the Constitution and not to a president. That is the law. That is what every officer in the quiet room at Quantico had sworn.

He finished fifth in his primary with nine percent of the vote. The generals who stayed silent in that room kept their careers. The lawyers who documented the machinery — the Former JAGs Working Group — were organized by men and women who had already been fired. The scholars who named the danger lost their board seats. The video that told the oversimplified version got seventeen million views. The documents that told the accurate version are in this file.

The American system does not need soldiers to save it. It needs its civilians — its legislators, its judges, its governors, its voters — to do their jobs so that the soldiers never have to choose. That is the whole argument. The next three weeks will test it.

## Chapter 9: The Money

### The diversion valve

Follow the money, and the first thing you find is that it does not stay in its lanes.

In December 2025, Senators Elizabeth Warren and John Garamendi, joined by eleven colleagues, released a report documenting that the Pentagon had spent more than $2 billion supporting the Department of Homeland Security's immigration operations in 2025 — without reimbursement. The breakdown: $1.3 billion for border operations including wall construction; $258 million for the city deployments in Los Angeles, Chicago, Portland, and Memphis; $420.9 million to convert military bases into detention facilities, including Guantánamo Bay and Camp Lemonnier in Djibouti; $40.3 million for military deportation flights; and $55 million for the reassignment of military lawyers to serve as immigration judges and federal prosecutors. A second reconciliation in June 2026 brought the DHS total to roughly $240 billion.

The money had to come from somewhere inside a defense budget that Congress had appropriated for defense. It came from readiness: the Vibrant Response exercise cancelled, a 10th Mountain Division training rotation cancelled, Arctic Sentry "descoped." It came from a $1 billion barracks renovation cancelled. It came from schools for military children in Kentucky and Germany, from a clinic at Whidbey Island, from a jet-training facility in Mississippi, from Marine barracks in Japan.

General Caine acknowledged the trade to Senator Warren in writing. The training that was cancelled was the training that prepares troops for war. The troops who were deployed were the troops who would otherwise have been conducting it. The 101st Airborne spent part of this period at the border instead of maintaining air-assault readiness. The California Guard was half-deployed during fire season. The 11th Airborne was pulled from Pacific deterrence to stand by for Minneapolis.

The Pentagon's fiscal year 2026 request added $5 billion for border operations. The fiscal year 2027 request, as documented by the Center for American Progress, added $3 billion for the urban-crime surge and $1 billion for Guard personnel — naming the D.C. and Memphis deployments as the model for a permanent program.

And in September 2025, the mechanism for building migrant detention through the Pentagon was formalized. The Navy's Worldwide Expeditionary Multiple Award Contract — WEXMAC — created in 2021 to support expeditionary forces in Afghanistan, Sudan, and Ukraine, was opened to DHS and ICE for detention construction. The ceiling was raised from $10 billion to $65 billion — a sixfold increase, made once ICE began using it. One hundred and twenty contracts followed. One went to GEO Group.

Senators Warren and Shaheen demanded the Pentagon end the arrangement, writing that DHS was using it to "sidestep the full federal acquisition process." On June 25, 2026, DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin told Congress: "We're not using it anymore." By then, the Government Accountability Office had documented $18.6 million in waste at a single facility — meals billed for five thousand people when the population was sixteen hundred, guards paid before the first detainee arrived — and found that "significant, pervasive issues" characterized the whole arrangement. Most WEXMAC offerors had no detention experience at all. The warehouse purchases themselves drew bipartisan scrutiny: the eleven sites were bought at an 11-to-13 percent premium over market, per Bisnow's analysis; Representative Rick Larsen, the Transportation Committee's ranking Democrat, said he had not seen documentation for any of ICE's detention-center purchases; and Republican Paul Gosar demanded answers about the Surprise, Arizona facility in his own district. DHS spent $11.36 billion in September 2026 alone — $6.25 billion of it construction, the largest single federal spending category that month.

### The companies that win

CoreCivic, the nation's largest private prison company, saw its stock rise 56% from the election through mid-2026. Its ICE revenue rose 96% year over year in the first quarter of 2026, to $261 million. It reactivated six dormant facilities — in Texas, California, Kansas, Tennessee, Oklahoma, and Virginia — worth a combined $665 million in annual revenue. The WEXMAC roster is the likely answer; the identity was never confirmed in the public record.

But the strangest transaction in the entire detention story was not a contract. It was a purchase. The Department of Homeland Security decided to stop renting and start buying. It purchased four CoreCivic facilities — 7,187 beds — for $2.2 billion. That works out to more than $300,000 per bed. According to the analysis by the investigative outlet inewsource, that is more than seven times the company's own book value for those same properties. On October 5, 2026, GEO sold two more facilities to DHS for $950 million, explicitly to escape California's oversight statutes, while retaining the management contracts.

The market structure makes this possible. Good Jobs First, analyzing federal procurement data, found that the number of unique companies receiving ICE contracts collapsed from 1,567 in 2008 to 448 in 2025 — a 71% concentration. GEO Group alone holds 45% of all ICE Security and Protection spending — $7.1 billion in cumulative ICE awards over seventeen years, against CoreCivic's $2.11 billion. And 155 contractors have collected $53 billion in penalties — for fraud, environmental violations, workplace safety, bribery — while earning $14.5 billion from ICE. Penalties, in this market, are a cost of doing business.

### Camp East Montana: the $1.2 billion that bought a scandal

The story of one facility tells the whole story of the procurement layer.

In June 2025, Immigration and Customs Enforcement solicited bids to build and operate a detention camp at Fort Bliss, Texas — on land where Japanese Americans were interned during World War II. The solicitation contained a clause requiring any contractor to notify ICE of any contact from Congress or the press. ICE tried twice to award the contract and failed both times. So the Army took it over — routing around the agency's own failed procurement.

On July 18, 2025, the Army awarded the contract — up to $1.3 billion over five years — to a company called Acquisition Logistics LLC. Acquisition Logistics was a 39-person firm headquartered in the suburban Richmond, Virginia home of its 77-year-old CEO, a retired Navy flight officer named Ken Wagner. Its largest prior contract had been $16 million. It had no detention experience. It qualified for the award through a set-aside program for veteran-owned and Hispanic-owned small businesses — an anti-DEI administration using a minority-preference program to sole-source a billion-dollar contract without meaningful competition.

Three days after the award, a 38-year-old worker named Hector Gonzalez was crushed to death at the site.

The facility that resulted was, by every documented account, a catastrophe. The Government Accountability Office found thirty-eight inspection deficiencies — the worst of any ICE facility reviewed. There were more than 130 emergency calls in five months. At least three detainees died. A measles outbreak occurred. A loaded firearm was lost. The Army used "lowest price technically acceptable" procurement without consulting ICE on what ICE actually needed — which is how the government ended up paying for meals for five thousand people at a facility holding sixteen hundred, and paying for guards, medics, and transport before the first detainee arrived.

In March 2026, ICE fired Acquisition Logistics — stating that the contract "did not accurately reflect ICE's operational needs." The replacement was Amentum, at $452 million plus a $776 million extension: another $1.2 billion. In Florida, the $245 million "Alligator Alcatraz" tent complex in the Everglades — where a federal report described detainees held in small metal cages — was ordered wound down by a federal judge even as the new construction awards were signed.

The market data for September 2026 tells you what this spending now means to the broader economy: federal spending on construction — the NAICS category that now includes detention construction — was $6.25 billion in a single month, the largest of any federal spending category. ### The enforcement gap

The ledger ends where it began: with the law's own machinery.
